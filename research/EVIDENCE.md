## A. Learning by teaching is established practice

### A1. Kobayashi meta-analysis: preparing to teach, then teaching
- **Source:** Kobayashi, K. "Learning by Preparing-to-Teach and Teaching: A Meta-Analysis." *Japanese Psychological Research* 61(3), 192–203. Online 2018-08-16; print issue July 2019.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/jpr.12221. Abstract read via the Crossref record: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/jpr.12221
- **Population:** 28 studies of learners acquiring domain knowledge. The abstract does not name an age band.
- **Quotes:**
  > "The synthesis of 28 studies indicated that the estimated effect sizes (Hedges' gs) were 0.35 for preparing-to-teach and 0.56 for teaching with preparing-to-teach."

  > "Both preparing-to-teach and teaching with preparing-to-teach were effective in promoting deep learning (as well as surface learning) and even after a delay."

  > "The learning benefits of teaching with preparing-to-teach were larger when students expected and engaged in an interactive teaching activity than when they expected and engaged in a non-interactive teaching activity."
- **Comparison group:** both effects are measured against "mere studying without teaching expectancy".
- **Status:** verified (opened).

**Follow-up by the same author:** Kobayashi (2019), "Interactivity: A Potential Determinant of Learning by Preparing to Teach and Teaching," *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:2755. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6336728/
- It reanalyzes the meta-analysis:
  > "Direct teaching after studying with direct teaching expectancy (relative to merely studying without teaching expectancy) had a larger beneficial effect, g = 0.84, than indirect teaching after studying with indirect teaching expectancy, g = 0.48, which in turn exceeded indirect teaching after studying without teaching expectancy, g = 0.23"
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Relevance to Pip:** teaching Pip is interactive, since Pip asks questions and takes a quiz. But Pip is not a face-to-face human. Pitch it as interactive teaching, and don't claim the g = 0.84 "direct teaching" figure.

### A2. Fiorella & Mayer (2013, 2014): expecting to teach vs actually teaching
- **Primary papers:**
  - Fiorella & Mayer (2013), "The relative benefits of learning by teaching and teaching expectancy," *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 38(4), 281–288. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.06.001
  - Fiorella & Mayer (2014), "Role of expectations and explanations in learning by teaching," *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 39(2), 75–85.
  - Both citations confirmed via Crossref. ScienceDirect blocked the abstracts.
- **Source opened:** Logan Fiorella's own chapter, "Learning by Teaching," in *In Their Own Words* (hosted by UNH, posted 2023). https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-learning-by-teaching-fiorella.pdf
- **Population:** college students learning about the Doppler effect.
- **Quotes:**
  > "On immediate comprehension tests, students who prepared to teach performed similarly, regardless of whether they actually taught on video. However, students who actually taught performed best on a delayed comprehension test."

  > "In a series of studies by Fiorella and Mayer (2013, 2014), undergraduate students who prepared to teach a lesson on the Doppler effect performed better on an immediate comprehension test, but not a delayed comprehension test, compared to students who prepared normally for a test."

  > "This finding suggests learning by teaching is most effective when used as a retrieval activity." (This is about Koh et al. 2018, who used the same materials.)
- **Status:** verified (opened). The text is the first author's own summary; the journal abstract itself was not opened.
- **Takeaway for Pip:** expecting to teach helps short-term. Actually teaching, from memory, is what lasts, and Pip makes the kid actually teach.

### A3. Nestojko et al. (2014): expecting to teach
- **Source:** Nestojko, Bui, Kornell & Bjork. *Memory & Cognition* 42(7), 1038–48. PubMed abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24845756/. The WUSTL press release is also verified: https://source.washu.edu/2014/07/expecting-to-teach-enhances-learning-recall/
- **Population:** "participants"/"students" in two lab experiments. Neither the abstract nor the release gives an age.
- **Quotes:**
  > "Participants expecting to teach produced more complete and better organized free recall of the passage (Experiment 1) and, in general, correctly answered more questions about the passage than did participants expecting a test (Experiment 1), particularly questions covering main points (Experiment 2)"

  > "Instilling an expectation to teach thus seems to be a simple, inexpensive intervention with the potential to increase learning efficiency at home and in the classroom."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Caveat:** the abstract gives no effect size, and there was no long-delay test.

### A4. Roscoe & Chi (2007): knowledge-building vs knowledge-telling
- **Source:** Roscoe & Chi, "Understanding Tutor Learning: Knowledge-Building and Knowledge-Telling in Peer Tutors' Explanations and Questions," *Review of Educational Research* 77(4), 534–574. DOI 10.3102/0034654307309920. Full-text PDF from ASU: https://education.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/rod_chi_rer_07_3.pdf
- **Quotes:**
  > "Prior research has established that peer tutors can benefit academically from their tutoring experiences. However, although tutor learning has been observed across diverse settings, the magnitude of these gains is often underwhelming."

  > "The review supports these hypotheses but also finds that peer tutors tend to exhibit a pervasive knowledge-telling bias. Peer tutors, even when trained, focus more on delivering knowledge rather than developing it."

  Definitions:
  > "reflective knowledge-building (e.g., monitoring of comprehension and knowledge) or knowledge-telling (e.g., summarization with little monitoring or elaboration), which should lead to stronger or weaker learning, respectively."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Why it matters for Pip:** this is the design rationale. Pip's quiz mistakes and follow-up questions push the kid from *telling* to *building*: monitoring and repairing their own rule. It is an honest caveat too, because teaching alone isn't magic.

### A5. Chase et al. (2009): the protégé effect with teachable agents
- **Source:** Chase, Chin, Oppezzo & Schwartz, "Teachable Agents and the Protégé Effect: Increasing the Effort Towards Learning," *Journal of Science Education and Technology* 18(4), 334–352. DOI 10.1007/s10956-009-9180-4. Stanford repository record: https://purl.stanford.edu/zm369yx3532
- **Population:** Study 1 was 8th graders learning biology; Study 2 was 5th graders.
- **Quotes:**
  > "Two studies demonstrate the protégé effect: students make greater effort to learn for their TAs than they do for themselves."

  > "TA students spent more time on learning activities (e.g. reading) and they also learned more. These beneficial effects were most pronounced for lower achieving children."

  > "Perhaps having a TA invokes a sense of responsibility that motivates learning, provides an environment in which knowledge can be improved through revision, and protects students' egos from the psychological ramifications of failure."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Closest prior art:** Betty's Brain, where students teach an agent that "can reason based on how it is taught." Pip is a direct descendant. The "protects students' egos" line supports the design choice that Pip makes the mistake, not the kid.

### A6. Common practice: peer tutoring and reciprocal teaching

**EEF Teaching & Learning Toolkit, Peer tutoring.** UK schools. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/peer-tutoring
- Headline rating: "High impact for very low cost based on extensive evidence", +6 months.
- Quotes:
  > "Peer tutoring approaches have been shown to have a positive impact on learning, with an average positive effect equivalent to approximately six additional months' progress within one academic year. Studies have identified benefits for both tutors and tutees, and for a wide range of age groups."

  > "There is a slightly higher impact (+7 months) for secondary age pupils than primary age pupils (+5 months)."

  > "A number of studies have been undertaken involving digital technology, with similar overall impact."
- **Status:** verified (opened).

**EEF Toolkit, Metacognition & self-regulation.** +8 months. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation
> "The average impact of metacognition and self-regulation strategies is an additional eight months' progress over the course of a year."

**Status:** verified (opened).

**EEF Toolkit, Reading comprehension strategies.** +7 months; this strand includes reciprocal reading. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/reading-comprehension-strategies
> "The average impact of reading comprehension strategies is an additional seven months' progress over the course of a year."

- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Note:** EEF has no stand-alone "reciprocal teaching" strand. Reciprocal reading sits inside this one, which EEF rates "moderate evidence" and partly discounts because "a large percentage of the studies were not independently evaluated."

**EEF general caveat**, printed on every strand page:
> "As with any evidence review, the Toolkit summarises the average impact of approaches when researched in academic studies."

**Hattie (Visible Learning).** Unofficial aggregator reproducing Corwin's December 2017 list: https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/
- Values: "Reciprocal teaching 0.74", "Peer tutoring 0.53", "Self-verbalization and self-questioning 0.55". The page also says: "Hattie found that the average effect size of all the interventions he studied was 0.40."
- **Status:** secondary only. The site is not Hattie's or Corwin's own.
- **Caveats to state if used.** Robert Slavin, "John Hattie is Wrong" (2018): https://robertslavinsblog.wordpress.com/2018/06/21/john-hattie-is-wrong/
  > "The essential problem with Hattie's meta-meta-analyses is that they accept the results of the underlying meta-analyses without question. Yet many, perhaps most meta-analyses accept all sorts of individual studies of widely varying standards of quality."

  Verified (opened).
- **Recommendation:** for a judge, prefer EEF's months-of-progress, which come with an evidence-strength rating, over Hattie's d values.

## B. Why explaining beats memorizing

### B1. Self-explanation: Chi et al. 1989 and 1994
- **Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser (1989),** *Cognitive Science* 13(2), 145–182. DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1. Abstract via Crossref.
  - Population: students studying worked mechanics examples, talk-aloud.
  - Quote:
    > "We find that "Good" students learn with understanding: They generate many explanations which refine and expand the conditions for the action parts of the example solutions, and relate these actions to principles in the text."

    > ""Poor" students do not generate sufficient self-explanations, monitor their learning inaccurately, and subsequently rely heavily on examples."
  - Status: verified (opened).
- **Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994),** "Eliciting Self-Explanations Improves Understanding," *Cognitive Science* 18(3), 439–477. DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3. Abstract via Crossref.
  - Population: 8th graders. n = 14 prompted vs 10 controls, who read the text twice.
  - Quote:
    > "Without any extensive training, 14 eighth-grade students were merely asked to self-explain after reading each line of a passage on the human circulatory system. Ten students in the control group read the same text twice, but were not prompted to self-explain. [...] The prompted group had a greater gain from the pretest to the posttest."

    > "High explainers all achieved the correct mental model of the circulatory system, whereas many of the unprompted students as well as the low explainers did not."
  - Status: verified (opened). The sample is small; cite it as foundational, not as an effect size.

### B2. Bisra et al. (2018) meta-analysis
- **Source:** Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne, "Inducing Self-Explanation: a Meta-Analysis," *Educational Psychology Review* 30(3), 703–725. DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x. Full-text PDF: https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf
- **Quotes:**
  > "identified 69 effect sizes (from 64 research reports) which met certain inclusion criteria. The overall weighted mean effect size using a random effects model was g = .55."

  > "a random effects analysis of 69 effect sizes (5917 participants) obtained an overall point estimate of g = .55 with a 95% confidence interval ranging from .45 to .65"
- **By education level** (moderator table, not significant, QB p = .384): Elementary g = .483 (k = 10); High school g = .426 (k = 13); Undergraduate g = .611 (k = 42).
- **Status:** verified (opened).

### B3. Retrieval practice: Roediger & Karpicke (2006)
- **Source:** "Test-Enhanced Learning," *Psychological Science* 17(3), 249–255. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x. Full text: https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf
- **Population:** Washington University undergraduates; prose passages.
- **Quotes:**
  > "When the final test was given after 5 min, repeated studying improved recall relative to repeated testing. However, on the delayed tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material." (abstract)

  > "In Experiment 2, students in the repeated-testing condition recalled much more after a week than did students in the repeated-study condition (61% vs. 40%), even though students in the former condition read the passage only 3.4 times and those in the latter condition read it 14.2 times." (General Discussion)
- **Design note:** conditions used the same number of 5-minute periods (SSSS vs SSST vs STTT), so this is an **equal-time** comparison.
- **Status:** verified (opened).

### B4. Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017) meta-analysis
- **Source:** "Rethinking the Use of Tests: A Meta-Analysis of Practice Testing," *Review of Educational Research* 87(3), 659–701. DOI 10.3102/0034654316689306. ERIC EJ1141817.
- **Quote** (abstract, via Crossref and ERIC):
  > "Results reveal that practice tests are more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions."
- **Status:** verified (opened) for the qualitative claim.
- **Overall g = 0.61 (272 effect sizes, 118 articles):** secondary only. I could not open the full text.

### B5. Dunlosky et al. (2013): utility ratings
- **Source:** Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham, "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58. DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266. Abstract via Crossref.
- **Quotes:**
  > "Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility assessments because they benefit learners of different ages and abilities"

  > "Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice received moderate utility assessments. [...] elaborative interrogation and self-explanation have not been adequately evaluated in educational contexts"

  > "Five techniques received a low utility assessment: summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text learning, and rereading."

  > "Most students report rereading and highlighting, yet these techniques do not consistently boost students' performance, so other techniques should be used in their place (e.g., practice testing instead of rereading)."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Honest note:** Dunlosky rates self-explanation *moderate*, not high. Pip combines self-explanation with practice testing (Pip's quiz plus the kid's correction), so say that, and don't overclaim self-explanation alone.

## C. Homework and time

### C1. Cooper, Robinson & Patall (2006)
- **Source:** "Does Homework Improve Academic Achievement? A Synthesis of Research, 1987–2003," *Review of Educational Research* 76(1), 1–62. DOI 10.3102/00346543076001001. Full text, UCR copy: https://assess.ucr.edu/media/766/download
- **Population:** US K–12 studies.
- **Quotes:**
  > "Studies that reported simple homework–achievement correlations revealed evidence that a stronger correlation existed (a) in Grades 7–12 than in K–6" (abstract)

  > "For students in elementary school, the average correlation between amount of homework and achievement was nearly r = 0; for students in middle grades it was r = .07; and for high school students it was r = .25." (summarizing Cooper's 1989 synthesis)

  > "For junior high students the positive relation appeared for even small amounts of time on homework (less than 1 hour per night) but disappeared entirely after students reported doing between 1 and 2 hours each night."

  > "Even for these oldest students, too much homework may diminish its effectiveness, or even become counterproductive."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Precision note:** "weaker in middle school" is right for the 1989 data (r = .07 middle vs .25 high school). The 2006 analysis split K–6 vs 7–12, not middle school separately. All correlations, not causal.

**EEF Toolkit, Homework.** UK schools. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/homework
> "The quality of the task set appears to be more important than the quantity of work required from the pupil. There is some evidence that the impact of homework diminishes as the amount of time pupils spend on it increases."

> "Studies in secondary schools show greater impact (+5 months) than in primary schools (+3 months)."

**Status:** verified (opened). The rating is "very limited evidence".

- Column: "Average hours spent per week doing homework", among "Students who do homework outside of school".
- 2019 rows:

  | Group | Hours per week |
  |---|---|
  | All students | 5.4 |
  | All elementary school students (kindergarten through grade 8) | 4.6 |
  | Secondary (grades 9 through 12) | 7.5 |

**Pew Research Center (2019-02-20),** ages 15–17, American Time Use Survey. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/02/20/the-way-u-s-teens-spend-their-time-is-changing-but-differences-between-boys-and-girls-persist/
> "Overall, teens (ages 15 to 17) spend an hour a day, on average, doing homework during the school year, up from 44 minutes a day about a decade ago and 30 minutes in the mid-1990s."

**Status:** verified (opened).

### C3. Understanding-based practice vs drill time
- **Best evidence:** Roediger & Karpicke (B3). Equal study time, but retrieval gave 61% vs 40% one-week recall while reading the passage 3.4 vs 14.2 times. Pair with Dunlosky's "practice testing instead of rereading" (B5).
- **What no study gives:** a number of homework hours or minutes saved by explanation-based practice in real K–12 homework. **I found none.** Pip's claim must be "shorter, more effective practice", not "saves X hours".

## D. Transparency for parents and tutors

### D1. Kraft & Rogers (2015)
- **Source:** "The underutilized potential of teacher-to-parent communication: Evidence from a field experiment," *Economics of Education Review* 47, 49–63. DOI 10.1016/j.econedurev.2015.04.001. Abstract on Brown Annenberg: https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/underutilized-potential-teacher-parent-communication-evidence-field-experiment
- **Population:** high school students in a summer credit-recovery program; randomized.
- **Quotes:**
  > "This experiment examined an intervention that delivered weekly one-sentence individualized messages from teachers to the parents of high school students in a credit recovery program. Messages decreased the percentage of students who failed to earn course credit from 15.8% to 9.3%—a 41% reduction. This reduction resulted primarily from preventing drop-outs, rather than from reducing failure or dismissal rates."

  > "messages emphasizing what students could improve, versus what students were doing well, producing the largest effects."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Relevance to Pip:** the most effective message type was "what to improve", which is exactly what a Pip report contains.

### D2. Kraft & Dougherty (2013)
- **Source:** "The Effect of Teacher–Family Communication on Student Engagement: Evidence From a Randomized Field Experiment," *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness* 6(3), 199–222. DOI 10.1080/19345747.2012.743636. Abstract via OpenAlex.
- **Population:** 6th and 9th graders in a mandatory summer school program.
- **Quote:**
  > "On average, teacher–family communication increased the odds that students completed their homework by 40%, decreased instances in which teachers had to redirect students' attention to the task at hand by 25%, and increased class participation rates by 15%."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Wording caveat:** "odds" of homework completion, not completion rate. The intervention was a daily phone call home plus a text or written message.

### D3. Bergman & Chan (2021)
- **Source:** "Leveraging Parents through Low-Cost Technology," *Journal of Human Resources* 56(1), 125–158. https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/56/1/125
- **Population:** middle and high schools, Kanawha County, West Virginia (22 schools, per J-PAL).
- **Quote:**
  > "We sent weekly automated alerts to parents about their child's missed assignments, grades, and class absences. The alerts reduced course failures by 27 percent, increased class attendance by 12 percent, and increased student retention, though there was no impact on state test scores. There were larger effects for below-median GPA students and high school students. More than 32,000 text messages were sent at a variable cost of $63."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Correction to the brief:** the figure is **27%**, not 28%. Also, no test-score effect.

### D4. Bergman (2021)
- **Source:** "Parent-Child Information Frictions and Human Capital Investment: Evidence from a Field Experiment," *Journal of Political Economy* 129(1), 286–322. DOI 10.1086/711410.
- **Published abstract** (via OpenAlex):
  > "I provide biweekly information to a random sample of parents about their child's missed assignments. Parents have upwardly biased beliefs about their child's effort. Providing information attenuates this bias and improves student achievement."

  Verified (opened).
- **Numbers, from the 2015 working paper (CESifo WP 5391):** https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo1_wp5391.pdf. Los Angeles high school students.
  > "For high school students, GPA increased by .19 standard deviations. There is evidence that test scores for math increased by .21 standard deviations, though there was no gain for English scores (.04 standard deviations). These effects are driven by several changes in students' inputs: assignment completion increased by 25% [...] Classes missed by students decreased by 28%."

  > "Parents in the treatment group contacted the school about this information 83% more often than the control group and parent-teacher conference attendance increased by 53%."
- **Status:** verified (opened), but these are **working-paper** numbers. The JPE version may differ, so cite as "Bergman, working paper".
- **Also note:** the paper says "There are no estimated effects on middle school parent or student outcomes," because of contamination.

### D5. EEF Toolkit, Parental engagement
https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/parental-engagement
- +4 months:
  > "Parental engagement has a positive impact on average of 4 months' additional progress."
- On text messages:
  > "An EEF programme which aimed to prompt greater parental engagement with their children's learning through text message alerts delivered a small positive impact at very low cost."
- **Caveat worth respecting:**
  > "Interventions designed to engage parents in homework are not generally linked to increased attainment."

  So Pip's parent report should *inform* parents, not turn them into homework tutors.
- **Status:** verified (opened).

**Important framing:** every D study is in **schools**, with outcomes like credits, failures and attendance. **None** measures tutoring-center renewals or parent retention. They support "parents act on specific, timely information", not "Pip raises renewals by X%".

## F. Competitors and the current workflow

Research was done by a helper agent that opened each vendor page. I spot-checked four quotes (Oases, Khanmigo, Google Classroom, Kumon UK) against the saved page text, plus the Mathnasium Littleton page myself. All are **verified (opened)** unless marked otherwise.

### F1. Spreadsheets and Google Drive
- **No survey exists** that I could find.
- **Evidence:**
  - The Mathnasium Littleton quote above: per-child Google Sheets, 75–90 min/day.
  - A customer testimonial on TutorBird's site (https://www.tutorbird.com/student-management/): "instead of having to slowly input all the details in a spreadsheet … which I did for years".
  - Vendor taglines: TutorBird "Say goodbye to spreadsheets"; TutorCruncher "Get rid of your spreadsheets"; Oases "Stop struggling with incomplete spreadsheets".
- These show the vendors *assume* spreadsheets are the incumbent. That is marketing, not measurement.

## G. The learning-styles myth

### G1. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008)
- **Source:** "Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9(3), 105–119. DOI 10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x. Abstract via Crossref.
- **Quotes:**
  > "The most common—but not the only—hypothesis about the instructional relevance of learning styles is the meshing hypothesis, according to which instruction is best provided in a format that matches the preferences of the learner (e.g., for a "visual learner," emphasizing visual presentation of information)."

  > "However, we found virtually no evidence for the interaction pattern mentioned above, which was judged to be a precondition for validating the educational applications of learning styles."

  > "Moreover, of those that did use an appropriate method, several found results that flatly contradict the popular meshing hypothesis."

  > "We conclude therefore, that at present, there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice."
- **The authors' own caveat:**
  > "it would be an error to conclude that all possible versions of learning styles have been tested and found wanting; many have simply not been tested at all."
- **Status:** verified (opened).

### G2. Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal (2015): a direct test using Pashler's design
- **Source:** "Matching learning style to instructional method: Effects on comprehension," *Journal of Educational Psychology* 107(1), 64–78. DOI 10.1037/a0037478. ERIC EJ1055886.
- **Population:** college-educated adults.
- **Quote:**
  > "Results demonstrated no statistically significant relationship between learning style preference (auditory, visual word) and instructional method (audiobook, e-text) for either immediate or delayed comprehension tests. Taken together, the results of our investigation failed to statistically support the meshing hypothesis either for verbal comprehension aptitude or learning based on mode of instruction (digital audiobook, e-text)."
- **Status:** verified (opened).

### G3. Newton & Salvi (2020): how common the belief is
- **Source:** "How Common Is Belief in the Learning Styles Neuromyth, and Does It Matter? A Pragmatic Systematic Review," *Frontiers in Education* 5. DOI 10.3389/feduc.2020.602451. Abstract via Crossref.
- **Quotes:**
  > "A number of reviews, going back to 2004, have concluded that there is currently no empirical evidence that this "matching instruction" improves learning, and it could potentially cause harm."

  > "We identified 37 studies representing 15,405 educators from 18 countries around the world, spanning 2009 to early 2020. Self-reported belief in matching instruction to Learning Styles was high, with a weighted percentage of 89.1%, ranging from 58 to 97.6%."
- **Status:** verified (opened).
- **Caveat:** the authors note most included studies "used convenience sampling with small samples".

### G4. Husmann & O'Loughlin (2019)
- **Source:** *Anatomical Sciences Education* 12(1), 6–19. DOI 10.1002/ase.1777.
- **Population:** 426 undergraduate anatomy students.
- **Quote:**
  > "Results demonstrated that most students did not report study strategies that correlated with their VARK assessment, and that student performance in anatomy was not correlated with their score in any VARK categories."
- **Status:** verified (opened).

**Pitch use:** "We don't label kids as visual or auditory learners; there's no good evidence that matching instruction to a 'style' helps (Pashler et al. 2008). Instead Pip records evidence: how each kid actually explains an idea, in their own words."

## 1. Pitch-ready lines

1. Students who prepare to teach and then teach learn more than students who just study, with an effect size of g = 0.56 across 28 studies, and the benefit lasts after a delay *(Kobayashi, Japanese Psychological Research, 2019)*.
2. Interactive teaching beats one-way explaining: the benefit of teaching was larger when students expected and did an interactive teaching activity *(Kobayashi 2019)*.
3. When 8th graders taught a computer character, they spent more time learning and learned more than when studying for themselves, and the gains were biggest for lower-achieving kids *(Chase et al., J. Sci. Educ. & Tech., 2009)*.
4. Peer tutoring adds about six months of progress in a school year, for tutors as well as tutees, and is rated high impact on extensive evidence *(EEF Teaching & Learning Toolkit)*.
5. Prompting students to explain their reasoning has a reliable, moderate-to-large effect, g = .55 across 69 effect sizes *(Bisra et al., Educ. Psych. Review, 2018)*.
6. Retrieval beats rereading at equal study time: a week later, students who practised recalling remembered 61% vs 40% for re-readers, who read the passage 14.2 times vs 3.4 *(Roediger & Karpicke, Psych. Science, 2006)*.
7. Rereading and highlighting, the most common study habits, are rated low utility; practice testing is rated high *(Dunlosky et al., PSPI, 2013)*.
8. More homework isn't automatically better: for junior-high students, the homework–achievement link disappeared beyond 1–2 hours a night *(Cooper, Robinson & Patall, RER, 2006)*.
9. Weekly one-sentence teacher messages to parents cut the share of students failing to earn credit from 15.8% to 9.3%, and messages about what to improve worked best *(Kraft & Rogers, Econ. of Educ. Review, 2015)*.
10. Automated parent alerts about missed work, grades and absences cut course failures by 27% and raised attendance by 12% *(Bergman & Chan, J. Human Resources, 2021)*.
11. US tutoring (with driving schools) is an $18.9B category of 176,000 businesses, with no company above 5% share *(IBISWorld, 2025 report)*.
12. Progress tracking is still manual: one Mathnasium center reports 75–90 minutes a day on busy days filling per-child Google Sheets *(Mathnasium of Littleton, center website)*. Say "one center", not "centers".

## 2. Do not say

- **Any "saves X hours of homework" figure.** No study measures hours saved by explanation-based practice. Say "shorter, more effective practice," backed by Roediger & Karpicke's equal-time result.
- **"Pip raises renewals / cuts churn by X%."** No public tutoring-center churn benchmark exists, and the parent-communication studies measure school outcomes (credits, failures, attendance), not renewals.
- **"Saves tutors X minutes per report."** No study. The 75–90 min/day figure is one center's anecdote; don't generalize it.
- **"$18.9B tutoring market"** without the driving-schools qualifier. Also don't say "2025" for the $18.9B figure; the page labels it 2026.
- **"28% fewer failures" for Bergman & Chan.** The paper says 27%. Also don't imply a test-score gain; it found "no impact on state test scores."
- **Bergman's ".19 SD GPA" as a JPE number.** It is from the 2015 working paper, for high school only, with no middle-school estimate.
- **"Learning by teaching doubles learning" or "90% retention".** No source in this file supports "learning pyramid" retention percentages (I did not check them), so leave them out.
- **Hattie's d values as settled fact.** They are secondary and methodologically criticized (Slavin 2018). Use EEF instead.
- **"Self-explanation is rated high utility."** Dunlosky rates it moderate. Practice testing is the high one.
- **Kobayashi's g = 0.84 "direct teaching".** That is face-to-face teaching of a human; Pip is not that.
- **"Teaching Pip boosts test scores by X."** No Pip outcome data exists yet. Present the research as the mechanism, and the pilot as how we'll measure it.
- **"No product tracks misconceptions."** Say "none of the tools we checked describe it on their own pages."
- **Specific center tuition prices** (Kumon $X, Mathnasium $Y) as fact. Only unsourced price-guide sites have them.
- **"Parents should help with homework" framing.** EEF finds interventions engaging parents in homework are "not generally linked to increased attainment". Pip informs parents; it doesn't make them tutors.
- **"Kids have learning styles; Pip adapts to them."** That contradicts section G.
